Modernizing the DCC Railroad Track Switch Thrower

Part of Princeton University’s engineering curriculum (MAE 412: Microprocessors and Their Applications) uses automated model train sets as the platform for students to design and test microprocessor control systems. With the original DCC railroad track switch thrower with a trickle-charged capacitor being ~20 years old, I analyzed the legacy schematic and designed, sourced, and hand-soldered a modernized single-sided PCB featuring an onboard ATMEGA-328P microprocessor and two 4700µF capacitors.

Engineering Analysis & Single-Sided PCB Redesign

1. Curriculum Context & Legacy Schematic Analysis Part of Princeton University’s engineering curriculum is a class centered on microprocessors and their applications. This class uses model train sets as the platform for students to create and test their own automated model train sets, utilizing the knowledge they have gathered from the class. One of the most useful tools given to students is a DCC railroad track switch thrower with a trickle-charged capacitor (DCC RR switch thrower). With the board’s design being ~20 years old and its limits becoming clear, it was decided to remake and modernize the board. First, I analyzed the schematic of the old board (Figure 1) to understand what each individual part does: it uses power supplied by the track (plus an optional 5V DC input) and trickle-charges a capacitor over about 12 seconds. When the relay is triggered, it releases the capacitor’s charge and throws the switch.
2. Modernized Single-Sided Design, Assembly & Deployment The new board serves the same purpose with two key additions: first, putting an ATMEGA-328P microprocessor directly on the board to eliminate external connections and allow the track to be more easily controlled and automated; second, adding a second 4700µF capacitor so more charge is stored and two switch actions can be done in swift succession. Unlike the original double-sided board, the new board had to be single-sided, making trace routing significantly more complex to design. It took about two weeks to finish the design of the new DCC RR switch thrower (Figure 2), and about one week to gather all the parts and solder the board together. After review by my superior, two notes were immediately corrected, and the board is now actively put to use.
